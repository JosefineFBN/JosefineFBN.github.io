Which countries can afford the green transition?

If someone asked you how the fight against climate change is going, what would be your answer? Let us ask you right now!

Authors
Josefine Fie Birkedal Nielsen, s194449
Mathias Grejs, s243641

Your answer might depend on which country you are in. Global emissions of Green House Gasses (GHGs) have been steadily increasing since measurements started in the mid 19th century. Consequently, global surface temperatures have increased more than 1 degree in the same period, leading to widespread changes in ecosystems. (IPCC, 2023) You might already know that, but what part does your country play?
With a dataset of the following countries: Bangladesh, Brazil, China, Denmark, Germany, Pakistan, Sweden, Switzerland & Uganda, this anaysis examines the reasons for development of CO2e emissions per capita.
Take a look at the visualization below. The European countries clearly emitted a lot more CO2e per capita in the past, while drastically reducing those numbers every decade. Germany, Switzerland, Denmark, and Sweden all reduced emissions per capita significantly, by more than half for the latter two countries. Impressive progress! Right?

Figure 1: Graphs for each countries showing CO2e emission per. capita over a 55 year period. The countries include western countries (Denmark, Sweden, Germany, Switzerland), emerging economies (Brazil & China), & developing countries (Pakistan, Bangladesh & Uganda)

Starting from much lower levels, Brazil, Uganda, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and China have all significantly increased emissions per capita. In the same period, Uganda has increased by around 80%, China has increased by more than 900%, surpassing the European countries in the past two decades. When looking at countries’ CO2e impact per capita, comparison is inevitable. The arising question when comparing is simple; why do countries differ in CO2e impact per capita? Why do some countries emit more than others? And, is it only the wealthy countries that can afford the green transition? Or could other factors explain the opposite trends?

What does the GDP index reveal?

Comparing only the countries total CO2e per capita provides a partial picture of the countries sustainable status. By including GDP as a parameter for economic activity, it becomes possible to compare countries’ environmental impact across different levels of economic development. Moreover, this analytic lens might potentially reveal whether green transition is only for wealthy western countries.

Figure 2: Graphs for each countries showing CO2e impact per capita related to the countries’ GDP per capita. The countries include western countries (Denmark, Sweden, Germany, Switzerland), emerging economies (Brazin & China), & developing countries (Pakistan, Bangladesh & Uganda)

Taking a closer look at the countries’ CO2e impact per. capita related the countries’ GDP per. capita, different patterns appear. Note that the x axis is on a logarithmic scale.
Three tendencies appear, one being the western countries’ CO2e impact per capita decrease while the GDP increase. The second tendency is for the emerging economies, where it is seen that the CO2 per capita increase over time as well as the GDP. The last tendency is for the developing countries and appears to be an increase in GDP with a smaller increase in CO2e per capita. Taking the GPD into consideration, it seems to be the western countries that manage to decouple economic growth from CO2e impact. Taking a closer look at the graphs, there seems to be a tipping point around 1000-10.000 USD per capita, where countries can afford the green transition and hereby decrease CO2e per capita. The question then becomes: Why is it only the western countries? Which tendencies affect how the western countries are able to reduce their CO2e per capita?

Energy sector

GDP is a parameter often used to measure prosperity of a country. According to Keri (2025) energy is a parameter with close connection to prosperity, given that energy represents the necessity to most economic activities.
Taking a look at the visualization below, the percentage distribution of energy production by different energy sources in Brazil and Denmark are illustrated. Even though the two countries have very different CO2e impact per. capita, the figure indicates two energy grid consisting of mostly renewable energy.

Figur 3: Brazil and Denmark's share of energy sources in their energy grid.

Looking at Denmark’s energy grid’s development from 1990 to now, major differences appear. As the majority of the energy grid previously relied on fossil fuels, the energy grid today primarily relies on renewable energy. Based on the percentage distribution, this transition from fossil fuel to renewable energy appear to be a relatively recent tendency, as the share of renewable energy increased from 31,9% in 2010 to 81,6% today.
Looking at the energy grid from Brazil the tendencies is entirely different. Categorizing hydro as a renewable energy, the share of renewable energy has been Brazil’s primary energy source since 1990. What is interesting here, is that fossil fuel has increased from 4,5% in 1990 to 14,2% today, illustrating an opposite tendency compared to Denmark.
However, even though Denmark and Brazil’s CO2e impact per capita is following entirely different tendencies, their energy grids must be considered similar as they are largely dependent on renewable energy.

One must dig deeper into the energy sector of Brazil and Denmark to examine whether energy plays a role for the impact per capita. A way of doing so is to look at the social behavior in relation to energy. The transport sector plays a crucial role in relation to a country’s energy grid, and might indicate whether the citizens’ behavior have transitioned as well.
Looking at a dataset of locations of gas stations (Overpass Turbo, n.d.) and EV charging stations (openchargemap.org, n.d.) in Brazil and Denmark, the citizens’ behavior reveals the comprehensive green transition. Taking a look at figure below, the share of EV charging stations is 55,6% in Denmark, whereas the charging stations account for 9,5% of the infrastructure in Brazil.

Figure 4: The share of EV charging station in Brazil and Denmark.

This dataset indicates that the green transition of the electricity grid is well embedded in the Danish society, and reflects interaction between supply and demand, which is evident in the large proportion of EV charging stations.

Despite Brazil’s green energy grid, the proportion of EV charging stations is surprisingly low. Whether this can be explained by the fact that only around 20-25% of the population has access to a car (Motor Vehicle in Use 2021, n.d.), the costs associated with electric vehicles, or priorities by the government remains unknown, but the data reflects a society in which the transport sector has not yet undergone a green transition.

The significance of education

Another parameter that is used to both measure and explain a country’s wealth is the educational system. But how does it look when comparing the educational system for a country that has decoupled the economic growth from the CO2e emissions, with a country that is in the developing phase and still has not? And can the educational system be used to explain the countries CO2e emissions indirectly?

To study this further, a look in to the Danish and Chines educational systems has been made. In the visualization below, a distribution of the educational level of the two countries populations can be seen. This visualization is based on the newest data from 2026.

Figure 5:The visualization shows the distribution of the educational level of the population of Denmark and China with the newest data from 2026.

The two countries has very similar distribution when it comes to the lower levels of education concerning the completion of primary and lower secondary school. But looking at the next levels of education it becomes clearer that there are differences between the two countries. The more developed country, Denmark, has more than double the amount of the population, looking at percentage points, than China, when it comes higher level of education than lower secondary school.

The reason for this difference in the distribution of the two populations educational level can of course be influenced by many parameters such as social heritage, cultural norms and governmental initiatives.
Parts of the governmental influence can be seen in the visualization below. Here the governmental expenditures on education as percentage of the country’s GDP can be seen. The graphs show that the Danish government spends a greater percentage of their GDP on their educational system than the Chinese government, which can explain why the Danish population has a higher level of education. This societal relation is further backed by studies, that explain that the governmental expenditures on education has a high influence on the amount of people that complete education on all levels (Hajebi et al., 2023).

With this visualization an even further insight into the society of these two types of countries can be gained. The visualization also shows the Gini index of the two countries, which can be seen as an indicator of economic inequality within a country.
The two graphs show that the inequality is higher in China than in Denmark. It furthermore shows, that the tendency in both countries governmental expenditures on education, is opposite for the tendency in the two countries Gini index. In Denmark there is a tendency that the government spends less on the educational system, but also a tendency to an increase in the Gini index. This opposite relation can be seen in China, where the government tends to increase their expenditures on the educational system while there is also a tendency to a decreasing Gini index.

Figure 6: This figure illustrate the GINI index and share of GDP used on education for Denmark and China overtime.

The study of the two visualization proved how social patterns concerning educational levels of a country can be explained through the governmental expenditures on the educational system. Furthermore, it could be seen how there appeared to be a relation between the governmental expenditures on education and the inequality of a society. This relation between a country's level of education and their economic situation, previously illustrated by GDP per capita, proves that a country that spends a larger share of the GDP on the educational system has a stronger economy based on GDP per capita. A population with a higher educational level has a great relation to economical growth, and can therefore have an indirect influence on the country’s reduction of emissions of CO2e.

The influence of comsumption

Another parameter to explore is by looking at the consumption patterns - do they match emissions trends? Would it be right to assume that high emissions also mean high consumption?

Figure 7: Household consumption per capita for the selected countries over time. Measured in Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) in 2021 constant $.

Going back since 1990, it is very clear that all the countries has had increasing household consumption over time, despite the large gap in levels between the countries. Side note: The European countries all show a clear drop in 2020, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic. Knowing this, what happens when compared to the CO2 emissions, which we know from earlier have been falling for the European countries?

Figure 8: Household consumption, PPP (2021 constant $) vs. CO2e (t) trajectores for the selected countries.

In this scatterplot, we see some very clear, but separate patterns. Overall, higher consumption and higher emissions seem to be tied together, with the points generally spreading from the lower left to upper right. The lower-income countries all appear on the left-hand side, with a clear trend that higher household consumption per capita also means higher CO2e emissions (or vice versa, but most likely consumption playing into the higher emissions. On the right, the Europeans all increased their consumption significantly, despite their simultaneous gradual decrease in emissions. The gap in consumption is simply explained by the selection of countries, with no middle-income countries represented.

Interestingly, China is an outlier from the countries with similar starting points in consumption. Since the beginning of the dataset, China had a much higher emission level than the other countries. Over time, this gap has grown significantly, while the country also increased its consumption levels from the low end to almost matching Brazil. What we see can to some extend be explained as "emissions outsourcing". Research has found that some developed countries, especially Anglophone ones (not included in this project), have, to some extent, been outsourcing their emissions by importing higher-emission products while exporting lower-emission ones. China is especially on the receiving end of this, to a greater extent than countries like Brazil, resulting in outsized per-capita emissions. The Nordics especially and European countries have been balancing reducing their emissions in a more balanced way, not outsourcing them (Baumert et al., 2019).

The limitations of data

While this study examine the CO2e impact per capita, it is important to emphasize misleading or inaccurate data for a comprehensive understanding. It is first of all important to distinguish between territorial and consumption-based emission:

  • Territorial emissions are emissions produced within a country’s border
  • Consumption based emissions are generated due to consumption regardless where the emissions are emitted (Ritchie, 2019)

This dataset measures CO2e impact per capita according to the territorial accounting method (CO₂ Emissions per Capita, 2025), which means that they do not account for emissions associated with increasing imports of goods nor outsourcing or shift in industrial production and indirectly relocate emissions – to countries such as China.

Figure 9: Map of the world, showing the percentage of manufactured goods of exported goods.

Looking at the times series, data for the selected countries shows the share of manufactured goods of merchandise exports, indicating how industrialized a country is. Taking a look at China, the share of exported manufactured goods almost double with an increase from 47% in 1984 to 91% in 2024.
Given the methodology behind the accounting of CO2e per capita, one possible hypothesis could be that the export of manufactured goods have shifted from the western countries to the emerging economies. A closer look at the figure shows that the western countries’ share of manufactured goods has remained relatively stable over the past 50 years, with Denmark being the only country to experience notable increase of approximately 26%.
The data does not reveal direct correlation between export developments in China and the western countries, given that the western countries share of manufactured good is relatively stable. Conversely, considering the previously observed increase in consumption in the western countries and their decrease in CO2e per capita, these trends do not interconnect. Taking China’s increase in consumption into consideration, this trend appears to begin around 2010. Taking a closer look at the visualization, it is seen that China’s export of manufactured goods is not influenced by China’ increase in consumption, and may therefore be a result of other countries import – e.g. reflect the increase in consumption in the western countries.

Trends influencing a country's ability to reduce emissions

The examination of CO2e per capita shows that trends influencing the impact are strongly depended on the country being examined. Following factors play a contributing role in countries’ impact.

  • Even though a country might produce renewable energy, its adaption must be integrated into the society in order to reduce the environmental impact.
  • Education may indirectly influence a country’s ability to reduce emissions. Government spending on education appears linked to educational attainment, equality and result in economic growth influencing the CO2e reduction.
  • A general tendency is observed in which countries with a decrease in CO2e per capita has an increase in consumption per. capita. Moreover, a tendency among countries with an increase in CO2e per capita is observed to have an increase in consumption as well.

This study’s data-foundation is based on territory accounting for CO2e per capita, and does therefore not directly show re-location of emissions due to shift in industrial activities.
This study suggests that the richer countries are better able to afford the societal transformation required to reduce emissions, including restructuring infrastructure and educational system, - all factors influencing their ability to reduce emissions. This study recommends to extend the analysis by including consumption based emissions in order to identify potential hidden and relocated emissions outsourced by global trade patterns influenced by consumption.
