Drawing on two decades of data, this analysis uncovers how assault and robbery are interconnected across San Francisco's urban landscape. Want to know where it's safe to explore—and where to stay cautious? Read on.

What does the GDP index reveal?

When looking at countries’ CO2 impact per. capita, comparison is inevitable. The arising question when comparing is simple; why do countries differ in CO2 impact per capita? Why do some countries emit more than others? And, is it only the whealthy countries that can afford the green transition?<

Comparing only the countries total CO2 per capita provides a partial picture of the countries sustainable status. By including GDP as a parameter for economic activity, it becomes possible to compare countries’ environmental impact across different levels of economic development. Moreover, this analytic lense might potentially reveal whether green transition is only for wealthy western countries.

Figure 2: Graphs for each countries showing CO2 impact per. capita related to the countries’ GDP per. capita. The countries include western countries (Denmark, Sweden, Germany, Switzerland), emerging economies (Brazin & China), & developing countries (Pakistan, Bangladesh & Uganda)

Taking a closer look at the dataset’s countries’ CO2 impact per. capita related the countries’ GDP per. capita, different patterns appear. Note that the z axis is on a logarithmic scale.
Three tendencies appear, one being the western countries’ CO2 impact per. decrease while the GDP increase. The second tendency is for the emerging economies, where it is seen that the CO2 per capita increase over time as well as the GDP. The last tendency is for the developing countries and appears to be an increase in GDP with a smaller increase in CO2 per capita. Taking the GPD into consideration, it seems to be the western countries that manage to decouple economic growth from CO2 impact. Taking a closer look at the graphs, there seems to be a tipping point around 1000-10.000 USD per. capita, where countries can afford the green transition and hereby decrease CO2 per capita. The question then becomes: Why is it only the western countries? Which tendencies affect how the western countries are able to reduce their CO2 per capita?

Energy sector

GDP is a parameter often used to measure prosperity of a country. According to Keri (2025) energy is a parameter with close connection to prosperity, given that energy represents the necessity to most economic activities.
Taking a look at figure X, the percentage distribution of energy production by different energy sources in Brazil and Danmark is illustrated. Even though the two countries have very different the CO2 impact per. capita, the figure indicates two energy grid consistent of mostly renewable energy.

Brazil and Denmark's share of enrgy sources in their energy grid.

Looking at Denmark’s energy grid’s development from 1990 to now, major differences appear. As the majority of the energy grids’ previously relied on fossil fuels, the energy grid today primary relies on renewable energy. Based on the percentage distribution, this transition from fossil fuel to renewable energy appear to be a relatively recent tendency, as the share of renewable energy increased from 31,9% in 2010 to 81,6% today.
Looking at the energy grid from Brazil the tendencies is entirely different. Categorizing hydro as a renewable energy, the share of renewable energy has been Brazil’s primary energy source since 1990. What is interesting here, is that fossil fuel has increased 4,5% in 1990 to 14,2% today, illustrating an opposite tendency compared to Denmark.
However, even though Denmark and Brazil’s CO2 impact per capita is following entirely different tendencies, their energy grids must be considered similar as they are largely dependent on renewable energy.

One must dig deeper into the energy sector of Brazil and Denmark to examine whether energy plays a role for the impact per capita. A way of doing so is to look at the social behavior in relation to energy. The transport sector plays a crucial role in relation to a countrie’s energy grid, and might indicate whether the citizens’ behavior have transitioned as well.
Looking at a dataset of locations of gas stations (Overpass Turbo, n.d.) and EV charging stations (openchargemap.org, n.d.) in Brazil and Denmark, the citizens’ behavior reveals the comprehensive green transition . Taking a look at figure X, the share of EV charging stations is 55,6% in Denmark, whereas the Chargin stations account for 9,5% of the infrastructure in Brazil.

The share of EV charging station in Brazil and Denmark.

This dataset indicates the green transition of the electricity grid is well embedded in the Danish society, and reflects interaction between supply and demand, which is evident in the large proportion of EV charging stations.
Despite Brazil’s green energy grid, the proportion of EV charging stations is surprisingly low. Whether this can be explained by the fact that only around 20-25% of the population has access to a car (Motor Vehicle in Use 2021, n.d.), the costs associated with electric vehicles, or priorities by the government remains unknown, but the data reflects a society in which the transport sector has not yet undergone a green transition.

The significance of education

Another parameter that is used to both measure and explain a countries wealth is their educational system (Måske mere spørgende?). But how does it look when comparing the educational system for a country that has decoupled the economic growth from the CO2 emissions, with a country that is in the developing phase and still has not? And can the educational system be used to explain the countries CO2 emissions indirectly?
To study this further a look in to the Danish and Chines educational systems has been made. In figure below, a distribution of the educational level of the two countries populations can be seen.

The vizulisation shows the distribution of the educational level of the population of Denmark and China.

The two countries has very similar distribution when it comes two the lower levels of education concerning the completion of primary and lower secondary school. But looking at the next levels of education it becomes clearer that there are differences between the two countries. The more developed country, Denmark, has more than double the amount of the population, looking at percentage points, than China, when it comes higher education than lower secondary school.
This difference is even great for the university educations, where Denmark has a distribution of 33.6% and 14% for people that have completed a bachelor and masters respectively, where as the values for China are 7.7% and 0.9%.

Parts of the governmental influence can be seen in graph XX. Here the governmental expenditures on education as percentage of the countries GDP can be seen. The graphs show that the Danish government spends a greater percentage of the GDP on their educational system than the Chinese government, which can explain why the Danish population has a higher level of education. This societal relation is further backed by studies, that explain that the governmental expenditures on education has a high influence on the amount of people that complete education on all levels (Hajebi et al., 2023).

This graphs an even further insight into the society of these two types of countries. The graphs also show the Gini index of the two countries, which can be seen as an indicator of economic inequality within a country.
The two graphs show that the inequality is higher in China than in Denmark. It further more shows, that the tendency in both countries governmental expenditures on education causes, is opposite for the tendency in the two countries Gini index. In Denmark there is a tendency that the government spends less on the educational system, but also a tendency to an increase in the Gini index. This opposite relation can be seen in China, where the government tends to increase their expenditures on the educational system while there is also a tendency to a decreasing Gini index.
