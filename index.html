Who can afford Green transition?

If someone asked you how the fight against climate change is going, what would be your answer? Let us ask you right now! Your answer might depend on which country you are in. Global emissions of Green House Gasses (GHGs) have been steadily increasing since measurements started in the mid 19th century. Consequently, global surface temperatures have increased more than 1 degree in the same period, leading to widespread changes in ecosystems. (IPCC, 2023) You might already know that, but what part does your country play?

With a dataset of the following countries: Bangladesh, Brazil, China, Denmark, Germany, Pakistan, Sweden, Switzerland & Uganda, tis anaysis examines CO2 emissions per capita.
Take a look at the map. The European countries clearly emitted a lot more CO_2e per capita in the past, while drastically reducing those numbers every decade. Germany, Switzerland, Denmark, and Sweden all reduced emissions per capita significantly, by more than half for the latter two countries. Impressive progress! Right?

Figure 2: Graphs for each countries showing CO2 impact per. capita over the past 50 years. The countries include western countries (Denmark, Sweden, Germany, Switzerland), emerging economies (Brazin & China), & developing countries (Pakistan, Bangladesh & Uganda)

Starting from much lower levels, Brazil, Uganda, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and China have all significantly increased emissions per capita. In the same period, Uganda has increased by around 80%, China has increased by more than 900%, surpassing the European countries in the past two decades. China is now above the world average, while only Germany among the selected European countries has not yet dropped below that level. When looking at countries’ CO2 impact per. capita, comparison is inevitable. The arising question when comparing is simple; why do countries differ in CO2 impact per capita? Why do some countries emit more than others? And, is it only the whealthy countries that can afford the green transition? Or could other factors explain the opposite trends?

What does the GDP index reveal?

Comparing only the countries total CO2 per capita provides a partial picture of the countries sustainable status. By including GDP as a parameter for economic activity, it becomes possible to compare countries’ environmental impact across different levels of economic development. Moreover, this analytic lense might potentially reveal whether green transition is only for wealthy western countries.

Figure 2: Graphs for each countries showing CO2 impact per. capita related to the countries’ GDP per. capita. The countries include western countries (Denmark, Sweden, Germany, Switzerland), emerging economies (Brazin & China), & developing countries (Pakistan, Bangladesh & Uganda)

Taking a closer look at the dataset’s countries’ CO2 impact per. capita related the countries’ GDP per. capita, different patterns appear. Note that the z axis is on a logarithmic scale.
Three tendencies appear, one being the western countries’ CO2 impact per. decrease while the GDP increase. The second tendency is for the emerging economies, where it is seen that the CO2 per capita increase over time as well as the GDP. The last tendency is for the developing countries and appears to be an increase in GDP with a smaller increase in CO2 per capita. Taking the GPD into consideration, it seems to be the western countries that manage to decouple economic growth from CO2 impact.
